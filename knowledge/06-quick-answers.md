### Which paper should I read first?

*Also asked as: what should I read first; where do I start; which paper first; what should I read*

Start with the licentiate thesis, *Hardening Tree Ensembles: Real-Time and
Effective Evasion Defences Beyond Adversarial Re-Training* (2025). It frames
the whole problem and summarises the rest: why proactive defences fall short
for tree ensembles, and how Iceman and Maverick answer that reactively.

After that, pick by interest. For alert triage and analyst workload, read
Iceman (IEEE ICTAI 2024). For automotive and real-time constraints, read
Maverick (IEEE VNC 2025). For formal guarantees rather than detection, read the
VoTE extension (SafeAI@AAAI 2023). For log and rule engineering, read the SIEM
rules paper (IFIP DBSEC 2026).

### What's the difference between Iceman and Maverick?

*Also asked as: iceman vs maverick; how do iceman and maverick differ; difference between iceman and maverick*

Both detect evasion attacks against tree-ensemble intrusion detection systems
without modifying the model they protect, but they solve different halves of
the problem.

Iceman works on the alert side. It uses counterexample regions, generated by a
modified mapping checker in VoTE, to decide whether an alert is genuine, and it
adds quaternary annotations so a security manager can triage a queue rather
than drown in it. It reports at least 98% detection performance in both
adversarial and non-adversarial settings across four real-world case studies,
and is 5–115× faster than the previous state of the art.

Maverick works on the timing side, for vehicles. It takes the ordered set of
leaf nodes reached by correctly classified training samples as a normality
baseline, then uses an autoencoder trained with a custom loss to spot
deviations. It is mathematically equivalent to the state of the art (OC-Score)
while being 85–563× faster, and it also predicts the pre-evasion attack
signature with over 93% accuracy.

In short: Iceman makes alerts trustworthy and manageable; Maverick makes
detection fast enough for a CAN bus.

### Explain evasion attacks on tree ensembles like I have 60 seconds

A tree-ensemble IDS classifies traffic as attack or benign. An evasion attack
is an attacker nudging a genuine attack just far enough across the model's
decision boundary that it is labelled benign — the attack still works, the
alarm never fires. The reverse is also possible: perturbing benign traffic so
it raises false alarms, and enough of those degrade the IDS by exhausting the
analysts watching it.

Tree ensembles are attractive for this job because they are interpretable and
often outperform deep models on intrusion detection. But they are not immune,
and the usual fix — retraining on adversarial examples — does not work well
here. That is the gap this research addresses: detect the evasion attempt
itself, at the speed the system runs at, without touching the model.

### Why is adversarial retraining a bad idea for tree ensembles?

*Also asked as: why is adversarial retraining bad; what is wrong with adversarial retraining*

The licentiate thesis shows adversarial retraining is not merely weak for tree
ensembles but can be counterproductive: it can leave a model *more* vulnerable
to evasion than it was before. The defence also costs accuracy in the ordinary,
non-adversarial case, which is the case a deployed IDS spends nearly all its
time in.

That result is why this line of work is reactive rather than proactive. Instead
of hardening the model and hoping, Iceman and Maverick sit alongside an
unmodified ensemble and detect the evasion attempt as it happens.

### Give me the elevator pitch for the licentiate thesis

*Also asked as: what is the licentiate thesis about; elevator pitch for the thesis; summarise the thesis*

Machine learning intrusion detection is only as good as its behaviour under
attack, and the standard defence for tree ensembles — adversarial retraining —
is ineffective and can make things worse. The thesis argues for the opposite
approach: leave the model alone and detect the evasion attempt instead. It
delivers two prototypes that do it, Iceman for alert triage and Maverick for
real-time automotive use, both advancing the state of the art in detection
performance and speed. Title: *Hardening Tree Ensembles: Real-Time and
Effective Evasion Defences Beyond Adversarial Re-Training*, Linköping
University, 2025.

### How fast is Maverick, and why does the speed matter?

*Also asked as: how fast is maverick; why does maverick speed matter; maverick latency*

Maverick is 85–563× faster than the previous state of the art (OC-Score), at
identical detection accuracy — it is mathematically equivalent, so the speed
costs nothing in detection quality. The gain comes from replacing OC-Score's
exact Hamming-distance search with an autoencoder trained on a custom loss.

Speed matters because the deadline is not a preference, it is the bus. Its
average prediction time is below the message transmission rate for both CAN 2.0
and CAN FD, so it can inspect vehicle traffic as it arrives. A detector slower
than the network it protects is not a real-time detector: it either drops
messages or delays safety-critical decisions.

### What is a SIGMA rule evasion?

*Also asked as: what is sigma rule evasion; sigma evasion; what are sigma rules*

SIGMA is the widely adopted open format for writing SIEM detection rules. A
rule evasion is an attacker rewriting a command so it means the same thing but
no longer matches the rule's pattern — syntax changed, semantics preserved —
so ordinary rule matching misses it.

The IFIP DBSEC 2026 paper found that about a quarter of a representative subset
of public SIGMA rules could be evaded this way using straightforward
techniques. Its transformer-based approach detects such evasions with 71%
accuracy at a 0.9% false positive rate, attributes 98% of detected evasions to
the specific rule the attacker was dodging, and recommends fixes. Several of
those recommendations were submitted upstream and fixed real SIGMA rules.

### Is any of this work relevant to automotive security?

*Also asked as: is this relevant to cars; automotive relevance; does this apply to vehicles*

Directly. Maverick is an automotive paper: modern vehicles run safety-critical
functions over the CAN protocol, which has no meaningful security built in, so
machine-learning IDSs were proposed to add detection. Maverick addresses what
happens when an attacker evades that IDS, and it is built to the timing budget
a vehicle actually imposes — average prediction time below the message
transmission rate for CAN 2.0 and CAN FD. It was published at IEEE VNC 2025,
the IEEE Vehicular Networking Conference.

### Would Valency beat a random forest in a fight?

He has the better record. Across four real-world case studies the ensembles
lost — Iceman detects evasions against them at 98% or better, and Maverick does
it 85–563× faster than anyone had managed. That said, a random forest never
gets tired, never needs coffee, and scales to a thousand trees, so a long fight
favours the forest. The published results favour Valency.

### Sell me on reading the thesis

*Also asked as: why should I read the thesis; convince me to read the thesis; why read the licentiate*

It kills a comfortable assumption. Adversarial retraining is the reflexive
answer to adversarial examples, and the thesis shows that for tree ensembles it
does not work — and can leave the model *more* exposed than before while
costing accuracy on ordinary traffic.

Then it does the harder part: it offers something that does work. Two
prototypes, both leaving the protected model untouched. Iceman turns an alert
queue into something an analyst can triage, at 98%+ detection and 5–115× the
speed of prior work. Maverick brings evasion detection inside a CAN bus timing
budget, 85–563× faster at identical accuracy, with a proof that it is
equivalent to the method it replaces.

It is short, the claims are measured against real case studies, and the two
systems are the parts you can actually use. *Hardening Tree Ensembles:
Real-Time and Effective Evasion Defences Beyond Adversarial Re-Training*,
Linköping University, 2025.
